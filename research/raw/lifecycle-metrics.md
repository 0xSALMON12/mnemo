## The Metrics That Define Player Lifecycle

10 min read- Share this module

## Retention: The Foundation Metric

Retention measures what percentage of players who installed on a given day are still active N days later. It's the single most important lifecycle metric because everything else — revenue, engagement, community health — depends on players actually coming back.

Standard retention intervals:

- D1 (Day 1): What percentage of players return the day after installing? This measures first impression quality. A low D1 suggests the onboarding experience, tutorial, or initial gameplay loop isn't compelling enough to bring players back

- D7 (Day 7): What percentage are still active after a week? This measures whether the core gameplay loop has enough depth to sustain interest beyond the novelty period

- D14 (Day 14): A mid-term checkpoint. Players still active at D14 have likely found lasting value in your game

- D30 (Day 30): The long-term retention benchmark. Players active at D30 are your core audience — the ones most likely to spend, recommend the game to others, and return for content updates

40–50%
Healthy D1 retention for live service / F2P titles — first impression and onboarding worked

15–30%
Healthy D7 retention — core loop has enough depth to survive past the novelty period

8–15%
Healthy D30 retention — your real audience: spenders, recommenders, returners for content updates

0.20+
DAU/MAU "stickiness" ratio that signals daily-habit territory — the players most likely to spend and stay

## What "Good" Retention Looks Like

The benchmark numbers above are aggregate averages for live service and F2P games. Real retention varies enormously by game type — comparing your roguelike's D7 retention against a mobile match-3 game is meaningless. Context matters:

- Competitive multiplayer / live service: D1 of 40–50%, D7 of 20–30%, D30 of 10–15% is strong

- Survival / crafting / sandbox: Often higher early retention (strong core loop) but steeper drop after D14 as content is exhausted

- Narrative / single-player: Lower D30 isn't a problem — players are meant to finish and move on. D7 that tracks completion rate is more meaningful

- F2P with daily mechanics: D1 of 35–45%, D7 of 15–25%, D30 of 8–12% is typical for well-performing titles

The most useful comparison isn't against industry averages — it's against your own game over time. Is D7 retention improving or declining with each update? Are players acquired this month retaining better or worse than last month's cohort? Attribution metrics from the previous path gave you the acquisition-side numbers; this is the post-install layer that tells you whether the channels are actually delivering players who stay.

## DAU, MAU, and User Stickiness

DAU (Daily Active Users) counts unique players who open the game on a given day. MAU (Monthly Active Users) counts unique players who opened the game at least once in the last 30 days. Both are useful, but the ratio between them is more informative than either number alone.

DAU/MAU ratio — often called "stickiness" — tells you what fraction of your monthly players are playing on any given day. A DAU/MAU of 0.2 means 20% of your monthly audience plays daily — the game is a regular habit. A ratio of 0.05 means players pop in occasionally but it's not part of their routine.

For marketing, stickiness matters because sticky players are more likely to:

- Make in-game purchases (more sessions = more opportunities to convert)

- Be active when content updates drop (they're already playing)

- Recommend the game to others (it's part of their daily life)

## Churn: Understanding Player Loss

Churn is the flip side of retention: the rate at which players stop playing. A 70% D7 churn rate means 70% of players who installed haven't returned after a week.

Churn analysis becomes actionable when you can identify when and why players leave:

- Early churn (D0-D1): Players leave during or immediately after their first session. Usually indicates onboarding problems, performance issues, or expectation mismatch (the game isn't what the ad promised)

- Mid-term churn (D3-D7): Players gave it a fair try but the core loop didn't sustain interest. Might indicate content depth issues or progression pacing problems

- Late churn (D14-D30): Players engaged for weeks but eventually dropped off. Often content exhaustion — they've seen everything the game currently offers. This is the cohort most responsive to content updates and reactivation campaigns

## Cohort Analysis

A cohort is a group of players defined by when they installed. Cohort analysis tracks how each group behaves over time, and comparing cohorts reveals trends that aggregate numbers hide.

Why cohorts matter for marketing:

- Campaign quality comparison: Players acquired during your Reddit campaign (March cohort) vs your Meta campaign (April cohort) — which group retained better? This directly informs channel allocation

- Update impact measurement: Did D7 retention improve after your latest content update? Compare pre-update and post-update cohorts

- Seasonal patterns: Do players acquired during Steam sales retain differently from those who bought at full price? This informs whether sale-driven growth produces lasting players

Cohort analysis is most useful when it's filtered by acquisition channel — so you can see retention curves for Meta vs Reddit vs creator-driven players side by side, not just one aggregate curve. The Retention Curve Shape Matters
Don't just look at specific retention day numbers — look at the shape of the curve. A steep early drop that flattens after D7 is healthy: you're losing casual browsers but retaining committed players. A gradual, steady decline with no flattening is concerning: even interested players are slowly losing engagement. The flattening point is your game's 'retention floor' — the core audience size your game naturally sustains.
